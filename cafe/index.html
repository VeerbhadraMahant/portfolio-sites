A day at Meherbaan

The café runs on the oven and the clock above the counter. This is what a normal day looks like, give or take a cricket match.

  1. 6:30am

    First batch of bun is out

    The back oven is lit at 4. By half past six the first trays are out and the biscuit jars on the counter are topped up. The bun is too hot to cut. Farokh cuts it anyway.

  2. 8:00am

    The morning regulars

    Same corner tables, same two orders. The retired uncles from Camp read two newspapers between them and disagree on both. Laptop people have till nine.

  3. 12:30pm

    Kheema for lunch

    The kheema has been on a low flame since morning. Shop owners from MG Road and office staff from nearby fill the marble tables for kheema pav and akuri on toast.

  4. 3:00pm

    Kitchen rests, chai doesn’t

    Hot food stops at three and starts again at six. Chai, biscuits and mawa cake carry on. It is the quiet hour: ceiling fans, cool marble, someone asleep over a book.

  5. 6:00pm

    The college crowd

    Chicken puffs come out again and the long table fills with students from the colleges nearby. One chai is often shared between two. Nobody is hurried along.

  6. 10:30pm

    Last chai, chairs up

    Chairs go up on the tables. Zenobia counts the day at the old counter while Farokh scrubs the chai kettle himself. Tomorrow’s dough is mixed and left to rest.

Three generations behind one counter

If we write the recipe down, somebody will try to improve it.

Farokh, on the chai

Rustom Irani left Yazd as a young man, spent a few years in a Bombay Irani café learning what to do and what not to, and in 1961 rented a corner shop in Camp with a borrowed oven and nine chairs.

He named it Meherbaan, which means kind. The sign painter added an extra a, Rustom liked it, and it stayed. The teak counter he brought over on a handcart is still the till.

In 1961 a cup of chai cost ten paise. The original price board still hangs behind the counter, old numbers and all, and nobody is allowed to touch it.

The bentwood chairs, the marble tops and the checkered floor are the originals, repaired more times than anyone has counted. The chai recipe has never been written down, so there is nothing to lose in a drawer.

  1. 1961

    Rustom Irani

    Opens Café Meherbaan. Stands at the chai kettle himself every morning until his knees say otherwise.

  2. 1994

    Farokh

    The grandson takes the counter at nineteen. He learns the chai by standing next to Rustom for five years and being corrected.

  3. 2019

    Zenobia

    Farokh’s daughter comes back from hotel school and takes over mornings, the books, and the WhatsApp orders. She has not touched the recipe.

How we make it

No machines in the chai. These are the two things people ask about most.

The chai

  1. Milk goes on first. Full-cream milk simmers slowly in the wide pan until it thickens and turns a little golden. It is not hurried.
  2. Tea is boiled apart. The leaves cook in their own kettle, so the decoction can be strong without making the milk bitter.
  3. Mixed to order. Decoction first, then the reduced milk, then sugar. Say “kam meetha” if you want it less sweet.
  4. Poured hot. It comes in a cup and saucer, hot enough to make you wait a minute.

The bun maska

  1. Dough rests overnight. It is mixed late in the evening and left alone.
  2. Baked before dawn. The first trays come out of the back oven near 6:30.
  3. Cut while warm. Split along the side, not all the way through.
  4. Butter, then more butter. Cold butter, spread thick to the edges. Dunk it in the chai. That is the correct way.

House rules

Posted by the door. Amended whenever somebody gets clever.

  • Laptops welcome till 9 am. After that, the table belongs to the chai.
  • Sit gently: the bentwood chairs are older than your father.
  • Dunking is allowed. Opinions about other people’s dunking stay in your head.
  • Eating alone? Share a table. Half our regulars met that way.
  • Less sugar: say “kam meetha”. “No sugar” needs a longer conversation.
  • Chai before photographs, please.
  • Cricket arguments are free of charge. Bill arguments are not.
